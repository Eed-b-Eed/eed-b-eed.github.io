When Learning Arabic Creates Jobs

Whether you want to improve your Arabic, learn it from scratch, or support the development of this initiative, build it Hand-in-Hand (Eed-b-Eed in Arabic) with us!

Among the unsung heroes of Palestinian society are local women. When the world in this troubled Land is falling apart, for some literally and for others metaphorically, these resilient women keep their families going day by day, whatever the circumstances. Their quiet pragmatism is heroic.

To keep your family going, you need income. Where paid work exists, there are areas of Palestine where job opportunities for local women, including those holding university degrees, are still very few. This is the case in al-Khalil, the southern West Bank region, known in Hebrew and English as Hebron.

The ancient city of Al-Khalil / Hebron is cherished by Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike for its sacred site associated with the burial place of Abraham, known in Arabic as Ibrahim, a figure of shared reverence among the three traditions. It is also the most populous governorate of Palestine (nearly 885,000 people), and its local Colloquial Arabic, known as Khalili, is the most widely spoken variety of Palestinian Arabic.

This initiative contributes to creating paid work opportunities for educated Khalili women that are
✓ financially sustainable,
✓ legally compliant,
✓ transparently funded,
✓ ethical,
✓ apolitical,
✓ culturally appropriate,
✓ socially acceptable,
✓ feasible for young parents.

Helping learners of Arabic as language support partners is such an opportunity, especially when carried out online and part-time, with arrangements that respect both local customs and traditions and the agency of each partner.

Learn Arabic. Create Jobs. Build Eed-b-Eed >Hand-in-Hand with us.

Learn - How does it Work?

Your learning journey with Khalilis

We believe we have come up with a solution to a significant challenge to online education in the West Bank.

The unpredictability of the situation on the ground means that it is very difficult for local people to work according to a regular fixed schedule. This is especially true for young parents in families with many children, or people who suffer from chronic illnesses and require frequent unexpected visits to the doctor. The lack of a timetable in public transport (if you had a chance to visit Palestine privately rather than on an organised trip, recall the famous orange minibuses called servees or ford) makes being “on time” extremely challenging, especially for those who do not have a car or do not drive. Sudden power outages and internet disruptions can further make carefully planned online activities simply unrealistic.

That’s why your learning journey will blend guided, structured self-study with flexible live sessions. These will be offered regularly by any one of our Khalili language support partners, depending on availability and circumstances. Due to their shared background and family ties, the Arabic they speak is very similar, so whichever partner practises with you, you will receive very similar training (subject, of course, to strong local personalities!). The family arrangement of this kind also allows for comfortable communication between the partners, especially when urgent personal matters arise and they need to swap slots at the last minute. This is why local family collectives are so powerful for teaching an authentic living variety of Arabic in a sustainable way.

This arrangement also aligns with circumstances of many prospective Eed-b-Eed learners. Our preliminary surveys and initial conversations indicate that many will be young professionals, often also with children. They too need workable flexibility, without compromising the quality of their learning.

For how this will work in practice, check out the sections above in their given order: Study ➡ Train ➡ Read ➡ Write ➡ Listen ➡ Speak.

Note: If you represent an organisation and would like its staff to benefit from Khalili experience in a more rigidly structured way over a specific period of time, please get in touch with the Eed-b-Eed Academic Coordinator, Dr Jakub Zbrzezny, at jakub.zbrzezny@abdn.ac.uk to arrange a call. A more structured arrangement possible, but it requires careful planning to avoid creating unrealistic expectations for our Khalili partners. We will be happy to discuss with you and our partners what is feasible.

Learn - Train

It's all about patterns

Arabic is characterised by surprisingly regular patterns. They will initially look somehow complex, but you will soon discover that they are intuitive and predictable. In the Section Train you will find tools for practising the most common patterns in names of actions (verbs), in names of objects (pronouns and nouns), in sounds, and, eventually, in other language categories. You will be able to tailor the practice to your current level of Arabic, training, if you wish, Khalili Arabic and Classical Arabic side by side.

The verbs are very common: write, speak, and say. The patterns include such popular everyday forms as ‘I don’t write’, ‘you say it’, ‘she speaks to him’, among many others (2,200 forms in total). Actions

Verb Trainer

Discover the joys of juggling Arabic words that describe actions (verbs). How do you say “I wrote it”, “Speak!” or “I didn’t say it”? Eed-b-Eed Beginners are provided with clear visual clues on how to pronounce the words, and more advanced Learners can challenge themselves with jumping across Khalili Arabic and Classical Arabic, to even switching off key pronunciation clues.

You can currently play with a selection of examples. All 2,200 forms that our Khalili Team Members have recently prepared will be available soon. That’s enough practice for more than one afternoon!

An accessible version of the Verb trainer will be designed in due course.

Objects

You and me

Here you will practise some of the most common words in Arabic like “I”, “you”, “he”, “she” etc. (pronouns), both on their own or in combination with other common short words such as ‘to’, ‘from’, ‘by’ (prepositions). We will soon post some exercises. Later, you will find here tools for practising how names of individual objects (nouns) in different forms, like “book” vs. “books”.

Sounds

Scottish lochs and Arabic

Like any language, Arabic has among its sounds certain troublemakers for non-native speakers. Fear not. With practice, you will overcome the challenge of producing sounds you may have never imagined yourself making. You will also discover why knowing how to pronounce a Scottish loch can make your life in Arabic easier.

Learn - Speak

Speaking Hand-in-Hand

In the Section Speaking you will be able to arrange individual or small-group coaching sessions with your Khalili language support partners. During the sessions you will actively practise all that you have learnt through other sections.

The most advanced Eed-b-Eed Learners will gradually progress to the live co-reading of Modern Standard Arabic or Classical and Quranic Arabic texts “Hand-in-Hand” with Colloquial Arabic in its Khalili variety. Even if mastery of Arabic is not attainable in the course of a few years of part-time study, in this way, you will come very close to experiencing how a native speaker roams freely across diverse manifestations of Arabic, from Quranic to Colloquial, today and in the past.

Questions & Answers

Q: Where is al-Khalil?

A: Known in English and Hebrew as Hebron, the city of al-Khalil is located in the southern West Bank, Palestine, approximately 40 km (25 miles) south of Jerusalem (al-Quds). In Arabic, al-Khalil refers both to the city and to the wider surrounding area, which constitutes the most populous governorate of Palestine. A Khalili refers in Arabic to a person who comes from there. It is also used to refer to the distinctive Palestinian variety of Arabic spoken in the region. Al-Khalil is famously associated with the traditional burial place of Abraham (Ibrahim), a figure revered in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

Q: Will Arabic native speakers understand me when I speak rural Khalili Arabic?

A: First of all, if you speak any of the Arabic dialects of the Middle East, you may find it difficult to communicate with native speakers of North African dialects from countries such as Morocco or Algeria. Many Arabs in such situations would switch to English too.

However, if you speak with people living in the Middle East, then yes, you will easily be able to maintain a good conversation. Our rural Khalili partners have not grown up in linguistic isolation. They all studied at universities, surrounded by many different local dialects, and successfully completed their degrees. Yet they resisted the social pressure to make their speech sound more “urban”, “prestigious”, or “educated”, as some people, including certain trained native and non-native teachers of Arabic, might perceive it.

This gives you a very rare opportunity to pick up authentic dialect features. Rest assured, using phrases or pronunciation associated with rural Palestine will often make your Arabic interlocutors smile widely and ask how you have learnt to speak like that. It’s a great icebreaker.

Q: How big is unemployment among Palestinian women in al-Khalil?

A: According to official governmental statistics, 82% of Khalili women aged 15+ are not in labour force. Of those 18% that are able to seek jobs, 35.80% face unemployment.

In approximate absolute numbers: with the estimation of the Khalili population at 885,00 people, and assuming a simplified 1:1 ratio between men and women, there will be around 442,500 in al-Khalil. Assuming that 60% of these are under the age of 15, around 177,000 can enter labour force. Of these, some 145,140 are not able to do so, remaining outside labour force. Among 31,860 who are able to seek jobs, some 11,400 cannot find employment. In total, this means that among Khalili women aged 15+, only 20,500 are employed, against 156,500 without employment.

Q: Is Eed-b-Eed associated with any local or international organisations or institutions outside the University of Aberdeen?

A: Stemming from long-term friendships, Eed-b-Eed works with trusted local individuals. It is not associated with any organisations or institutions in Palestine, Israel, or elsewhere. Any such association would require appropriate institutional approvals from the University of Aberdeen.

Q: Can I learn or collaborate with Eed-b-Eed regardless of my nationality, ethnicity, or religion?

A: Yes. It is the independent and generous choice of our Khalili partners to welcome all genuine learners or collaborators, regardless of their nationality, ethnicity, or religion.

Q: What is the status of Eed-b-Eed in 2026?

A: In this early stage, Eed-b-Eed is developing through research and knowledge exchange projects led by Dr Jakub Zbrzezny as a lecturer at the University of Aberdeen and the Enterprise and Innovation Champion in its School of Divinity, History, Philosophy and Art History. Think of the University as an incubator for this initiative. In due course and subject to appropriate approvals, Eed-b-Eed may take the form of a continuous professional development course or evolve into a Scotland-based registered charity (a not-for-profit organisation) or a University social enterprise (a company whose profit is reinvested into its mission), "hand-in-hand" with our Khalili partners.

Our Team

Khalili Team Members

At the heart of Eed-b-Eed is the family collective of five siblings from al-Khalil (Hebron). These are four sisters – A. Hroub, B. Hroub, R. Hroub, and S. Hroub – and their brother Ahmad Hroub.

They come from rural al-Khalil, and currently live across the Bethlehem and al-Khalil regions. Proud speakers of Khalili Arabic in its rural form, they are passionate about making their language thrive, amidst the linguistic pressures related to urbanisation, electronic communication, and AI, all of which lead to the gradual disappearance of local language varieties, and, subsequently, of the cultural traditions they carry.

All five siblings hold degrees from Palestinian universities, including Birzeit University, in fields including Arabic language and Literature, Law, and Economics.

For the past four years the siblings have been working as an institutionally contracted team in research projects led by Dr Jakub Zbrzezny at the University of Aberdeen, enabled by internal university funding and external grants from academic institutions such as the British Academy, Royal Society of Edinburgh, or Cornell University’s Department of Near Eastern Studies. Their work concerns pre-modern Arabic translations of Jewish and Christian Scriptures and their contemporary understanding among Muslim communities. You will see Ahmad co-presenting with Jakub at conferences and seminars, in person or online.

Building on Ahmad’s extensive experience in privately teaching Khalili Arabic, the siblings have started to develop didactic materials for Eed-b-Eed, thanks to grants from the Knowledge Exchange and Innovation Fund of the Scottish Funding Council and from the UK-based Al Tajir Trust, both administered by the University of Aberdeen. In due course, Eed-b-Eed learners will meet the siblings during live practice sessions. In future, the Hroub sisters will facilitate the recruitment of further local team members from among university-educated local women, and Ahmad’s training in economics will significantly help in making this model sustainable in the local ecosystem.

Why AI-free Learning and Teaching?

Can you learn Arabic from a chatbot?<

The first question to ask is: What kind of Arabic do you want to learn or practise?

On the one hand, if your goal is to learn or practise Modern Standard Arabic, the school-taught language of official media, law, and scholarly literature, you may benefit from interacting with chatbots powered by Large Language Models. This is because these models are built – or trained as computer scientists would say – predominantly on written and oral expressions of Modern Standard Arabic, widely available online.

On the other hand, if your goal is to learn Colloquial Arabic, which is the actual mother tongue of any Arabic native speaker, the situation dramatically changes.

Now, ask any popular chatbot if it knows Colloquial Arabic, it will assure you it does. Indeed, it will confidently start producing content that is meant to represent different dialects, that is, regional types of Colloquial Arabic. On the surface, the content will resemble major families of Colloquial Arabic. It may well look Egyptian, or North-African, or Levantine, that is, representing countries from the eastern coast of the Mediterranean. Your chatbot may well claim that its outputs reflect how people speak or write in specific areas – in Cairo, or perhaps Morocco, or Syria.

In fact, if you look at it more closely, you will see that such content is nothing more than an artificial mixture of many different dialects from a particular region. In other words, no Arabic native speaker would speak or write in this way.

What about Khalili Arabic?

This is precisely the case of Khalili Arabic. Thanks to a research grant from the Royal Society of Edinburgh, we conducted extensive experiments on how Artificial Intelligence handles it. In this investigation we teamed up with Computer Scientists from the University of Aberdeen.

We asked several popular chatbots to translate selected texts from English or Modern Standard Arabic into the rural Khalili Arabic. We prompted the chatbots to generate 200 texts, each of about 250 words, which were meant to represent the dialect of our Palestinian partners. In total, this was about 50,000 words, which is like a medium-size book.

Then, our Khalili team members scored each text – or evaluated in the computer science jargon – on how closely it actually resembles their dialect.

Not a single text felt to our evaluators as reflecting the rural variety of Khalili Arabic, and less than 10% were recognisable as Khalili in general. Between 10% and 30% texts felt as representing unspecified Palestinian dialects. All other texts felt as Levantine or simply Colloquial. This is why you will not learn a specific dialect from a chatbot, and this is why we are not using Artificial Intelligence for creating any teaching content here. It's AI-free learning and teaching.
